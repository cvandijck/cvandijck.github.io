Mass Personalisation of Preoperative Planning for Total Knee Arthroplasty

Teaching computers the shape of the human knee, so that every patient can get a surgery plan made for them.

PhD in Biomedical Engineering · KU Leuven & Materialise · 2021

Read the thesis

A worn-out knee

The knee is where the thigh bone (femur) meets the shin bone (tibia), with the kneecap (patella) in front. It carries your weight from hip to ankle while bending and straightening. A thin layer of cartilage on the bone ends lets them glide and spreads the load.

In osteoarthritis this cartilage slowly breaks down until bone rubs on bone. The bones deform, movement stiffens and pain becomes chronic. It is linked to age and overweight, so the numbers will keep rising.

people affected worldwide

Replacing the joint

When conservative treatment is no longer enough, surgeons perform a total knee arthroplasty: both worn contact surfaces are replaced by metal implants with a plastic spacer in between.

Osteoarthritis often pushes the leg into a bow-legged (varus) or knock-kneed (valgus) position, which shifts the load and wears the cartilage faster. The surgeon therefore tries to restore a healthy alignment of the leg.

Classic

Mechanical

Align the implant neutrally to the leg's load axis for an even load on the joint. The same target for everybody.

Population

Anatomical

The average knee is a few degrees into varus, so aim for that. Better, but still an average over a population.

Personal

Kinematic

Use the patient's own motion axes, corrected for the damage done by osteoarthritis. This needs the cartilage.

Planning the surgery

With a preoperative plan the surgeon studies the procedure in advance and takes the patient's specific anatomy into account: which implant, which size, where to cut. Planning has evolved from acetate templates held over an X-ray, to digital 2D templating, to a full 3D model of the patient's knee. 3D-printed, patient-specific guides then carry the plan into the operating room.

Personalised care for everyone?

Mass personalisation means personalised treatment, available at scale. Three trends make it urgent:

  • Knee replacements are booming: from about 700,000 a year in 2014 to a projected 3.5 million by 2040.
  • Patients are younger and expect more from their knee — they still want to play padel every evening.
  • Health systems, such as the UK's Getting It Right First Time programme, push for better-planned surgery.

But personalised planning needs personal data, and acquiring and processing that data does not scale easily.

Three barriers

1. Manual processing

Scans must be turned into 3D models and marked up with anatomical reference points. That costs expert time and does not scale.

2. Radiation

CT is cheap and widely available, but uses ionising radiation, and a planning scan has to include the hip.

3. Cartilage needs MRI

MRI shows cartilage, which is key for kinematic alignment and well-fitting guides, but it is slow, costly and less available.

The idea

All three barriers come down to missing shape information. What if we could predict it instead of measuring it?

A statistical shape model (SSM) learns from many example bones how a shape varies across a population. It consists of one average shape and a handful of variation modes, independent "sliders" found by mathematics. Any bone can then be described by just a few numbers: where each slider is set.

Think of the character creator in a video game, except that the sliders are not "nose size" but whatever the data says varies most.

Derive. Reconstruct. Augment.

A shape model describes a whole population, yet my hypothesis was that it can be used to predict missing information for one patient: derive information from the shape, reconstruct what was not scanned, or augment what is available with what is missing. One application per barrier.

First, the tools

A 3D bone is a cloud of points joined into triangles. To build a shape model you need correspondence: every bone must have the same number of points, and point n must sit at the same anatomical spot on every bone. I built two tools for this.

Warping

I improved a warping algorithm that deforms a template onto each training bone. The resulting models are as good as the state of the art, which only works for specific shapes, whereas mine works for any anatomy.

Fitting

I also built a fitter that finds the slider settings that best explain a new bone, even when part of it is missing. Tests with artificially removed surface show that the error grows with the missing area, but stays limited unless the bone end itself is gone.

Since then, colleagues and I have reused both tools for the leg, forearm, vertebra, scapula, pelvis, heart and aorta.

Result 1 · Barrier: manual work

Derive: landmarks, automatically

Surgeons plan with reference axes, such as the mechanical axis from hip to ankle. These axes follow from landmarks: points that engineers place by hand on each 3D bone. This takes clinical training and time, and it is exactly the kind of step that does not scale.

I first measured how well people do it. Between engineers, the average difference is below 1 mm, but occasionally a point is off by more than a centimetre — an error that could harm a patient if it slips through quality control.

Then I let the shape model do it: landmarks are treated as just another part of the shape that is "missing", and the fitter fills them in. The result is comparable to manual work, without outliers. Most derived axes are as accurate as manually derived ones.

Honest limit: the epicondylar axis, defined by two small bumps on the knee, is not accurate enough yet. Axes vary smoothly across people, which a model captures well; a single landmark can differ abruptly between individuals, which it does not.

Result 2 · Barrier: radiation

Reconstruct: scanning less

The mechanical axis runs from the hip joint to the ankle, so a knee CT normally includes the hip, along with radiation-sensitive tissue. Cutting the scan off halfway the femur would spare them, but then the hip is missing.

The bottom half of the bone alone does not tell the model enough. But every CT scan starts with a free topogram: a flat, X-ray-like overview image. From it we take two measurements (femoral neck angle and femur length) and the 2D positions of three landmarks.

The shape model is conditioned on the measurements, which shrinks the space of plausible bones. The 2D landmarks become 3D lines pointing straight out of the topogram, and the model is fitted so that its landmarks lie on these lines.

The outcome: without the topogram the femur comes out far too short. With it, the orientation of the mechanical axis in the front view is as accurate as the manual process. In the side view it is less reliable, because nothing constrains it there.

Result 3 · Barrier: MRI-only cartilage

Augment: adding the cartilage

Cartilage matters twice. Guides that rest on the cartilage surface sit more stably (today surgeons often remove all cartilage first, which makes the surgery longer), and kinematic alignment needs cartilage to find the patient's pre-disease motion axes. Both normally require an MRI.

Cartilage thickness is not random: it follows the bone's shape. So I built models that combine bone and cartilage, for each bone and for the whole joint, and asked the model to predict the cartilage from a CT-derived bone.

Two strategies work: predict the cartilage model's sliders from the bone's sliders (explicit), or fit one combined model to the bone and let it fill in the cartilage (implicit). A final correction step makes the cartilage follow the real bone exactly. Modelling the bones together with their relative position needs far fewer details to reach the same accuracy.

Outcome: clearly better than assuming an average thickness, and within the variability of manual segmentation. Remaining error hotspots are at the kneecap joint (the kneecap is not yet in the model) and on patient-specific wear that is hard to predict.

Take-home message

Shape models can derive, reconstruct and augment patient-specific shape information, helping to make personalised knee planning available at scale.

There is a catch. A model describes a population, and it only predicts reliably when:

  1. the information behaves smoothly across people, rather than jumping from one individual to the next, and
  2. the model can be constrained enough to give not just a plausible but the correct shape for this patient.

That is also an opportunity: machine and deep learning can help find the constraints, while shape models, which need far less data, can make up for machine learning's hunger for it.
